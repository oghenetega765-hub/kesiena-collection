#  *The friend request* 

# There are some people you meet and forget.

# Some conversations disappear the moment you put your phone down, sliding out of memory the way water slides off skin, then one second, gone the next, leaving no trace that it was ever there at all. You could sit across from someone at a wedding, laugh together for twenty minutes over jollof rice and small talk, exchange numbers out of politeness, and by the following week not even remember the shape of their face. Some names become nothing more than names you once knew, buried somewhere beneath hundreds of other memories, filed away in a drawer you never open again, gathering the particular kind of dust that only forgotten things gather.

# And then there are people who enter your life so quietly that you don't realize they're changing it until it's already too late. They don't announce themselves. They don't knock loudly or make grand entrances, the way you might expect something significant to arrive, with music swelling somewhere in the background, with some small shift in the air that tells you to pay attention. They simply slip in through a crack you didn't know was open, so unremarkably that you don't even feel the draft, and by the time you notice them standing inside your life, they've already rearranged the furniture, moved things around in a way you can't quite put back, even if you wanted to.

# I didn't know which one he would be.

# At the time, he was just a name on my screen. A stranger. Nothing more.

# At least, that's what I told myself, sitting there in the ordinary hush of an ordinary evening, with absolutely no reason to suspect that anything about that particular Tuesday or whichever unremarkable day it was deserved to be remembered at all.

# It was one of those slow evenings at the University, when time seemed to move differently, the kind of evening where the hours stretch and sag the way an old rope stretches under a weight it wasn't quite built to carry, where even the air outside my window felt heavy with waiting, thick with the smell of dust and the distant hum of generators kicking on one after another across the hostel block as the estate's power dipped for the evening. Somewhere down the corridor, I could hear the muffled bass of someone's phone speaker playing Afrobeats a little too loudly, and further off, the occasional burst of laughter from a group of girls gathered near the stairwell, their voices rising and falling in that particular rhythm of gossip exchanged in hushed, delighted tones.

# I was sitting in my room close to the window, where a thin strip of orange evening light was slanting in through the gap in my curtains, falling across the cover of a book open in front of me, my phone in my hand, the two objects arranged on my reading table like props in a play I wasn't really performing, doing what I had become increasingly good at doing: pretending to study while scrolling through my phone. The book stayed open to the same page for almost an hour. I don't think I read a single line of it that night. My eyes would drift down to the paragraph, skim across the words without absorbing a single one of them, and then drift right back up to the small glowing rectangle in my palm, over and over, in a loop so automatic I barely noticed myself doing it.

# School wasn't exactly easy for me then. Neither was home.

# There was a lot happening around me, and even more happening inside me, the kind of noise that doesn't come from outside but from somewhere underneath your ribs, humming quietly no matter what you're doing, the way a fridge hums in the background of a quiet kitchen, so constant you almost stop hearing it, except you never actually do. Things at home were falling apart in ways I didn't always know how to explain, not even to myself, let alone to anyone who asked. Phone calls with my mother had started carrying a particular strain in them lately, a carefulness in how we chose our words, as though we were both walking across a floor we weren't sure would hold our weight. I wasn't making many friends in school either. Somehow, the university that was supposed to feel like a new beginning, a clean page, sometimes felt like just another place where I was trying to figure out where I belonged, wandering the corridors of my own life like a visitor who had lost her map, nodding politely at people whose names I hadn't quite learned yet, eating lunch sometimes with people I liked well enough but didn't yet trust with anything real.

# Even church had become complicated.

# I had stopped going for a while. Not because I had suddenly stopped believing — my faith hadn't left me, not exactly — but because I was confused. Confused about life. Confused about people. Confused about myself, about the girl I saw in the mirror some mornings who didn't always feel like someone I recognized, someone whose eyes looked a little more tired than they should have at nineteen, whose smile in photographs had started to look, even to her, slightly rehearsed.

# I was tired. Tired in the way that sleep doesn't fix, the kind of tired that settles into you regardless of how many hours you spend lying in bed, the kind that has nothing to do with your body and everything to do with whatever's happening underneath it.

# Maybe that was why I opened Facebook that evening.

# I want to be honest about how ordinary the moment actually was, because I think it's important to understand just how little significance it carried at the time. I had never really been a Facebook person, actually my account existed mostly as a formality, something I'd created years earlier during secondary school because everyone else was doing it, and had since let go mostly dormant, checked maybe once every couple of weeks out of habit rather than genuine interest. I didn't have any particular reason for opening it that day. I don't even remember what exactly made me log in, whether my thumb moved before my mind decided, the way habits sometimes act faster than intention, my fingers finding the little blue icon among the rows of apps on my screen almost without my permission.

# Maybe it was boredom, the particular, heavy boredom of an evening with nothing scheduled in it, no assignment due the next morning urgent enough to demand my attention, no phone call planned, nothing but the slow, unhurried stretch of hours between now and whenever sleep finally decided to claim me.

# Maybe curiosity, some small, idle wondering about who from secondary school might have posted something new, some photograph from a wedding or a birthday that would give me five minutes of mildly entertaining distraction.

# Maybe I simply needed something to occupy my mind, something to drown out the noise that had been building all week, layer upon layer of small stresses stacking themselves up until they became a single, low, persistent hum I couldn't quite silence with anything else I tried.

# Sometimes, when life becomes too loud, you look for noise somewhere else. A different kind of noise. One you can control, one you can close with the press of a button if it ever becomes too much  unlike the noise at home, unlike the noise in my own head, which followed me around regardless of how many tabs I closed.

# So I opened Facebook.

# The blue and white interface loaded slowly, the network in my hostel being what it was, and I sat there for a moment scrolling absently through my notifications, nothing important, a birthday reminder for someone I barely remembered, a suggested post from a page I'd liked years ago and forgotten about entirely.

# And there it was.

# A friend request.

# Alex

# There were a lot of friend request showing in my tabs as I kept swiping.

# But one drew my attention 

# I paused, my thumb hovering just above the screen, the way you pause slightly when you catch sight of something out of the corner of your eye that doesn't quite match your expectation of the room.

# Something about the name caught my attention, the way a single word in a crowded sentence can suddenly pull your eye and hold it there, refusing to let you continue reading until you've gone back and examined it properly. It wasn't just the name, either. His profile picture looked strangely familiar too familiar in that unsettling way where you can't immediately place why, and your mind keeps circling back to it like a tongue returning to a loose tooth, unable to leave well enough alone even though prodding at it doesn't actually help anything.

# I sat up a little straighter, propping my phone against my knee, tilting the screen slightly to catch better light from the window, studying the small thumbnail image the way you might study a face across a crowded room, trying to place it.

# He reminded me of one of my coursemates.

# Not exactly. The eyes were slightly different, the smile a touch too wide in the photo, but enough to make me look twice. Enough to make me tilt my phone slightly, as if a different angle might explain the resemblance, the way you tilt a photograph to catch the light differently, hoping the change in angle might resolve some detail that's currently blurred.

# And that particular coursemate had been showing interest in me around that period. It wasn't anything dramatic  nothing he'd said outright  but I had noticed it, even if I pretended not to, the way you notice things you'd rather not have to respond to just yet. He wanted to know more about me, and I had noticed it in the glances that lasted a second too long during group work, in the questions that circled a little too close to things I hadn't offered to share, in the way he always seemed to find a reason to walk in my direction after class ended.

# So when I saw this stranger with a familiar-looking name and a face that reminded me of someone I knew, I became curious. It felt less like meeting someone new and more like being handed a puzzle with a piece already missing, a small itch of confusion that made me want to sit with it a little longer rather than simply scroll past.

# I almost ignored the request.

# Almost.

# My thumb actually drifted toward the small grey "delete" option beside his name, hovering there for a moment, weighing the mild effort of accepting against the mild effort of dismissing  the kind of inconsequential decision you make dozens of times a day without ever suspecting that one of them might turn out to matter.

# But maybe that's the funny thing about life. Sometimes, the biggest chapters of your life begin with something so small that you don't even recognize it as a beginning. You expect beginnings to arrive with some kind of warning, some shift in the air, a sign that says pay attention, this matters, the way a storm announces itself with a change in the color of the sky, with a particular stillness before the wind picks up. But most of the time, they don't. Most of the time, they slip in disguised as nothing at all, dressed exactly like every other unremarkable moment surrounding them, so that only much later, looking back, can you finally point to the exact second everything quietly shifted.

# For me, it was just a friend request.

# I accepted it, a single tap, unremarkable, my thumb already moving toward closing the app entirely, my attention already halfway back to the book I still wasn't actually reading.

# I didn't think much of it afterward. I set my phone down on the edge of my bed, picked up my book again, tried once more to pretend I was studying, my eyes tracing the same stubborn paragraph I'd been failing to absorb for the better part of an hour. The evening remained slow, the generator outside, it was mine neighbor's sputtering on with its familiar cough, it had a way of generating a fume that can cause untimely death, whenever it was turned on I just knew it was time to shut the windows, even the great amount of mosquitoes moving around won't make me close my windows but their generator did,the smell of someone's dinner jollof rice, maybe, or fried plantain was drifting faintly down the corridor and under my door.

# It was maybe twenty minutes later, or  perhaps closer to half an hour. I,  genuinely can't say for certain, because I wasn't watching the clock, wasn't waiting for anything when my phone buzzed against the mattress beside me.

# He sent a message.

# I saw the small red notification appear at the top of my screen, and for a moment I simply looked at it without reaching for the phone at all, the way you might glance at a knock on your door before deciding whether you're in the mood for visitors. The notification sat there on my screen for a while before I let myself open it, my curiosity building slowly rather than arriving all at once, the way steam builds gradually under a lid before finally lifting it.

# I didn't reply immediately. There was something about responding to a complete stranger that felt unnecessary, maybe even a little unwise, the ordinary, sensible caution that most of us carry, in theory, about people who appear out of nowhere on the internet. But boredom has a strange way of making strangers seem interesting, of turning small curiosities into things worth chasing just to see where they lead, the way a single loose thread on a sweater tempts you to pull it, even though some quiet, practical part of you already suspects you shouldn't.

# So eventually, after finishing the paragraph I'd been staring at for the better part of an hour without registering a word of it, I typed back.

# Politely. Carefully. The kind of reply you give when you're testing water with one foot, not yet ready to commit the rest of yourself a short greeting, a question that gave away almost nothing about me, the conversational equivalent of a handshake rather than an embrace.

# That was all it was supposed to be.

# A simple conversation.

# Nothing serious.

# Nothing worth remembering.

# At least, that was what I thought, setting my phone face-down on the table and finally, genuinely, returning my attention to my book, the strange little itch of curiosity from earlier having, for the moment, been satisfied.

# The conversation that followed didn't happen all at once, the way it might seem if you only look back at it from a distance and see two weeks compressed into a single, smooth impression. It happened slowly, in small pieces, spread out across ordinary days the way rain gathers gradually in a bucket left outside  a message here, a reply there, hours sometimes passing between one text and the next, neither of us in any particular hurry.

# That first night, we exchanged only a handful of messages before I fell asleep with my phone still in my hand, the screen eventually dimming and going dark against my palm. The next day, a short message arrived sometime in the late morning nothing dramatic, just a simple good morning, the kind of message anyone might send to someone they'd only just started speaking to. I didn't reply until much later that evening, after my last class, walking back to my hostel under a sky already beginning to soften into dusk.

# And it continued like that, day by day, the messages arriving with no particular urgency, no pressure behind them, each one small enough that I never had reason to feel uneasy about any single conversation on its own. It was only when I began to notice the pattern forming across several days that anything about it started to feel worth paying closer attention to.

# For the next two weeks, Alex was surprisingly easy to talk to.

# He asked about my day, not the way people ask out of habit, already half-listening for the next thing to say, their eyes already drifting toward their own phone screen mid-conversation, but the way people ask when they actually want the answer, following up on small details I'd mentioned days earlier, remembering things I hadn't expected anyone to remember. He asked questions about me, real questions, the kind that required me to think before I answered, that made me pause with my thumbs hovering over the keyboard, genuinely considering how to put something into words rather than reaching for whatever reply came easiest. He paid attention to little things the small, forgettable details I mentioned once and assumed would be forgotten, the way I'd once complained, only in passing, about a particular dish in the kitchen, only for him to bring it up again a full week later, as though it had actually mattered to him.

# Sometimes, I found myself wondering how he managed to know certain things about me. Some of his guesses landed with an accuracy that felt almost too convenient, too well-aimed to be chance the kind of accuracy that makes you sit up a little straighter, your eyebrows drawing together slightly, even as your thumbs keep typing a perfectly normal reply.

# Every ordinary guess landed like a magic trick.

# And every time, a small voice inside me whispered: That's not a guess. That's a script someone already read.

# I didn't know what to make of it. I would sit there staring at his messages, half-smiling, half-suspicious, caught between wanting to enjoy the conversation and wanting to understand exactly why it felt so unnervingly easy, the way you might enjoy a magic show while also quietly trying to work out where the coin actually went.

# There was something familiar about the way he talked. The way he asked questions, phrasing them in a particular rhythm that tugged faintly at some corner of my memory. The way certain things about him seemed to overlap with the person I thought he was small overlaps at first, the kind you could explain away as coincidence, a shared taste in music, a similar sense of humor, and then more of them, arriving one after another, until the explaining started to feel like work, until I had to actively remind myself that people could simply have things in common without it meaning anything at all.

# So I started paying closer attention.

# I began reading his messages a second time before replying, holding them up mentally against what little I actually knew about my coursemate, checking for the small, specific details that would either confirm or dissolve the growing suspicion sitting at the back of my mind.

# A day came, in the middle of an otherwise unremarkable conversation about something forgettable, he told me his name.

# Not just Alex.

# He said he had five names, the way many of us do, names stacked one after another through tradition, through family, through whatever occasion had called for another one to be added.

# I found that surprisingly funny, the idea of a person walking around with five names stitched to him like a coat with too many pockets, each one presumably carrying its own small story, its own reason for being chosen. I even sent back something teasing about it, and he laughed, or at least typed the text equivalent of a laugh, and for a moment the conversation simply continued along its ordinary, pleasant course.

# And then he mentioned one of those names.

# My coursemate's name.

# I stared at my screen, my thumb frozen mid-scroll, the ordinary hum of the room around me  the fan, the distant generator, the faint murmur of a conversation happening somewhere down the hall  seeming, for a moment, to fade slightly into the background as my attention narrowed entirely onto that single word sitting there on my screen.

# Again?

# At that point, the coincidence was becoming too strange to ignore, too specific to file away as chance. I started wondering if I had somehow been talking to the same person all along  if this whole thing was some elaborate, slightly absurd game, and I was the last one to find out the rules, the way you sometimes feel when you finally understand a joke everyone else in the room had already laughed at minutes earlier.

# Maybe the profile picture had been misleading.

# Maybe he had another account.

# Maybe there was some explanation I hadn't figured out yet, some thread I hadn't pulled, some perfectly ordinary reason two people could share so much without actually being the same person.

# So I investigated.

# I set my phone down for a moment, sat back against my head
